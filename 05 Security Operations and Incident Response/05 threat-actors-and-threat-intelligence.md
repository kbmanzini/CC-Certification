# Threat Actors and Threat Intelligence

## 1. What Is a Threat Actor?

A threat actor is an individual or group that may attempt to compromise systems, networks, or information.

Threat actors have different motivations, resources, and capabilities.

## 2. Common Threat Actor Categories

| Threat actor | General description |
|---|---|
| Cybercriminals | Seek financial gain |
| Nation-state actors | Operate in support of national interests |
| Hacktivists | Act to promote political or social causes |
| Insiders | Employees or trusted individuals who misuse access |
| Competitors | May seek unauthorized business information |
| Opportunistic attackers | Look for easy targets |

These categories can overlap.

## 3. Threat Actor Motivations

Common motivations include:
- Financial gain
- Espionage
- Political influence
- Disruption
- Revenge
- Ideological goals
- Competitive advantage

Understanding motivation can help organizations assess risk.

## 4. Threat Actor Capabilities

Capabilities describe what a threat actor is able to do.

They may depend on:
- Technical knowledge
- Available resources
- Funding
- Access to tools
- Time
- Organizational support

A threat actor with substantial resources may present a different level of risk from an opportunistic individual.

## 5. Insider Threats

An insider threat involves someone with authorized access who causes harm intentionally or unintentionally.

Examples:
- An employee intentionally misuses confidential information.
- An employee accidentally shares a sensitive document.
- A former employee retains access that should have been removed.

Ways to reduce insider risk:
- Least privilege
- Separation of duties
- Access reviews
- Security awareness training
- Monitoring and auditing
- Timely removal of access

## 6. Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence is information analyzed to help an organization understand threats and make security decisions.

It can include:
- Threat actor information
- Known malicious domains
- Malware indicators
- Vulnerability information
- Attack trends
- Industry-specific risks

Raw information becomes more useful when it is analyzed and placed in context.

## 7. Types of Threat Intelligence

| Type | Focus |
|---|---|
| Strategic | High-level trends and business risks |
| Tactical | Adversary tactics and techniques |
| Operational | Information about specific campaigns or planned activity |
| Technical | Indicators such as IP addresses, domains, and file hashes |

## 8. Threat Intelligence Sources

Examples:
- Government cybersecurity advisories
- Trusted industry information-sharing groups
- Security vendor reports
- Internal security logs
- Vulnerability databases
- Information-sharing and analysis centers (ISACs)

Information should be evaluated for accuracy, relevance, and freshness.

## 9. Threat Intelligence Lifecycle

A common simplified lifecycle:

1. Planning and direction
2. Collection
3. Processing
4. Analysis
5. Dissemination
6. Feedback

The process helps ensure intelligence answers real organizational questions.

## 10. Threat Intelligence vs. Threat Hunting

- **Threat intelligence:** Information that helps an organization understand threats.
- **Threat hunting:** A proactive search for signs of threats that may not have triggered existing alerts.

## Key Takeaways

- Threat actors have different motivations and capabilities.
- Insider threats can be intentional or accidental.
- Threat intelligence turns information into useful security knowledge.
- Strategic, tactical, operational, and technical intelligence serve different purposes.
- Intelligence should be evaluated before it is trusted.
